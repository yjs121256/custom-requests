Is Your Korean Translation Actually Good? How to Review a Machine-Translated Website or App

Machine translation and AI tools have made it easy to put a Korean version of your website, app or Shopify store online in an afternoon. The hard part is knowing whether it's any good. If you don't read Korean, the text looks fine because you can't see the problems, and Korean visitors can.

This guide explains the most common issues in machine-translated Korean and how to check for them before launch.

Why "correct" isn't the same as "natural"

A machine translation can be grammatically correct and still feel foreign. Korean readers notice text that reads like a direct copy of English sentence structure, words that are technically right but never used that way, and tone that jumps around. For a store or app, that can make a brand feel less trustworthy, even if every word is "accurate".

Issue 1: Mixed politeness levels

Korean has different speech levels, and brands choose one on purpose. Two common choices:

  • 해요체: friendly and polite, common for consumer brands and apps.
  • 합니다체: more formal, common for official notices, finance and B2B.

Machine translation often mixes them: a friendly button label next to a stiff, formal error message. Pick one level and apply it everywhere. This single decision does a lot for how professional your Korean looks.

Issue 2: Broken placeholders and tags

Website and app text often contains variables like {{ name }} or %{count}, and HTML tags. Translation tools sometimes translate, move or break them, which can cause visible errors or crashes. Korean word order also differs from English, so the right place for a variable inside a sentence may change. Any review should check that every key, variable and tag survives exactly as it was.

Issue 3: Too literal

Phrases like "Add to cart", "Sign up for free" or "You're all set!" have natural Korean equivalents that shoppers already know from local sites. Literal translations stand out. A reviewer should replace them with the wording Korean users expect.

Issue 4: Inconsistent terms

The same English word may be translated three different ways across your site. Your product names, feature names and key terms should be consistent. Decide which brand terms stay in English and which get a fixed Korean spelling, and keep a short glossary.

Issue 5: Length and layout

Korean text can be shorter or longer than English depending on the phrase. Buttons, menus and mobile screens can overflow or wrap awkwardly. After editing, check the live pages, especially navigation, checkout and short UI labels.

Issue 6: Untranslated leftovers

Look for English that slipped through: email templates, error messages, checkout steps, image alt text and app store descriptions are often missed.

How to run a review

  1. Export the text in a reviewable format: a spreadsheet with source and Korean side by side, a JSON/strings file, or a document.
  2. Decide the tone (해요체 or 합니다체) and audience.
  3. List brand terms that must stay in English or have a fixed Korean spelling.
  4. Review line by line for tone, natural wording and consistency.
  5. Check placeholders and tags in every edited line.
  6. Keep a change log so you can see what changed and why.
  7. Re-import and check the live layout.
